01 / Background

Why this project: putting math to work on a real problem

What I love about math is how it turns a vague worry into a question you can actually answer. In A-Level Math and Further Math, I learned tools like hypothesis testing, probability models, and regression, but I almost always used them on textbook data. I wanted to see what they could do when I pointed them at a real-world problem.

I picked children's oral health because it's a problem everyone assumes they already understand. Tooth decay is one of the most common chronic conditions in children, and most of it is preventable with simple habits. So if people know what to do, why do so many kids still get cavities? Math can help answer that, by measuring the gap between what people know and what they do, and by testing which factors really matter.

My project asks three questions:

  1. Do students brush, floss, and see the dentist the way they know they should?
  2. Which factors are actually linked to good habits, and which only seem to be?
  3. What kind of oral health education would students actually pay attention to?

08 / Deeper analysis

Beyond the percentages

Percentages describe the sample, but they can't tell me how confident to be or whether two things are genuinely related. In this section I use statistical methods to test the patterns above.

A. How precise are these numbers?

With only 101 respondents, every percentage comes with uncertainty. I calculated 95% confidence intervals using the Wilson score method, which performs better than the standard textbook formula when a proportion is close to 0% or 100%.

p̃ = (p̂ + z²/2n) / (1 + z²/n),   margin = z·√(p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n²) / (1 + z²/n),   z = 1.96

Dot = sample percentage; line = 95% confidence interval (n = 101).

The intervals are wide, roughly ±10 percentage points, but some conclusions hold up anyway. Even at the upper end of its interval, the share of people getting regular check-ups is still under half (45%).

B. Building a habit score and a knowledge score

To compare respondents on a single number, I built two scores. The habit score (0–8) awards one point for each healthy habit: brushing at least twice a day, brushing every night, brushing for at least two minutes, using fluoride toothpaste, flossing at least sometimes, replacing the toothbrush every three months or sooner, not eating after the bedtime brush, and getting regular check-ups. The health facts score (0–4) awards one point for each correct answer on Q19–Q22.

E. Testing relationships

I used chi-square tests of independence to check whether pairs of answers are related, and Cramér's V to measure the strength of each relationship (0 = none, 1 = perfect).

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E,   V = √(χ² / (n · (k − 1)))

Since I ran seven tests, I used a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 0.05 / 7 ≈ 0.007 to guard against false positives from multiple testing. The two school-level tests have some expected counts below 5, so their p-values are approximate.

Only one relationship clears that bar: people who believe check-ups are very important are much more likely to actually go. Several relationships I expected didn't show up. Sugary drinks weren't linked to reported dental problems, possibly because damage takes years to appear and people don't recall it accurately. F. What predicts regular check-ups? A logistic regression

A chi-square test looks at one factor at a time. To weigh several factors at once, I fit a logistic regression, which models the log-odds of getting regular check-ups as a linear combination of the predictors.

What matters is whether someone believes check-ups are very important: those respondents have about times the odds of going regularly. The confidence interval is very wide because the sample is small, so the exact figure isn't reliable, but the direction is clear. Fear of the dentist isn't significant once belief is accounted for.

One caveat: this is a cross-sectional survey, a single snapshot in time. People who already go regularly may have learned from their dentist that check-ups matter, so the causation could run both ways.

10 / Limitations

Limitations

  • Not a random sample. I distributed the survey through my personal network, so the sample skews toward big cities, Guangdong and Hubei provinces, and high school seniors (36 of 101). The results describe this group, not students across China.
  • Small subgroups. Only 14 middle schoolers responded, so I don't draw firm conclusions about that group.
  • Self-reported data. People may overstate how long or how often they brush. Younger children likely answered with a parent's help.
  • A flaw in question design. Q18 asked, “If you don't go to the dentist regularly, why not?” but it was shown to everyone. 36 people reported regular check-ups in Q15, yet only 19 chose “I already go regularly” in Q18. Next time I'd use skip logic so only people without regular check-ups see Q18.
  • Limits of the scores and tests. The habit score has only moderate internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.58), so it's a rough summary. The regression has just 36 positive cases, which is why its confidence intervals are so wide. A larger sample would make these estimates much more precise.
  • Correlation, not causation. Everything comes from a single cross-sectional survey, so I can identify associations but not prove cause and effect.
  • Possibly rushed responses. people finished in under 90 seconds, and one open-ended answer was off-topic. I kept all responses because their multiple-choice answers looked consistent, but this may add some noise.

11 / Next steps

Next step: an AR brushing coach

The survey points to two problems a lesson alone can't fix: people don't brush long enough or thoroughly enough, and nobody can see their own brushing to check. So my next step is to design an AR brushing coach for elementary school kids and evaluate it with the same statistical approach I used here.

How it would work

  • Real-time tracking. A phone or tablet camera follows the position of the toothbrush and mouth while the child brushes. The mouth is divided into zones (for example, upper-left outer surfaces or lower-right inner surfaces), and the app logs how many seconds each zone gets.
  • AR feedback. An animated model of the teeth is overlaid on the screen. Each zone turns clean once it has been brushed long enough, while a two-minute timer counts down. The design is built around animation, the format respondents preferred most.
  • Check-up reminders. Short animations explain why to see a dentist even when nothing hurts, targeting the belief my regression identified as the key factor.
  • Reports for parents. A weekly summary shows brushing time, coverage by zone, and missed nights.

How I would test it

  • Recruit a group of elementary school students and track their habit scores and brushing time for two weeks before they use the app, then for two weeks with it.
  • Compare before and after with a paired test (Wilcoxon signed-rank). Because the app logs brushing time directly, I won't have to rely only on self-reports, one of the main limitations of this survey.
  • Run a power analysis beforehand to determine how many children I need to detect a meaningful change.

The first version will be a simple prototype built on existing open-source hand- and face-tracking tools. A working prototype is enough for the real question: does real-time feedback actually change how kids brush?
